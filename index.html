What is an Option?

An option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a fixed price (the strike) on or before a set date (the expiry). You pay a premium upfront for this right.

The Two Types

Call Option

Right to buy the underlying at the strike. You profit when the stock rises above the strike. Used to express a bullish view.

Put Option

Right to sell the underlying at the strike. You profit when the stock falls below the strike. Used to express a bearish view or hedge.

Key Terms

  • Strike (K): The fixed price at which you can buy/sell.
  • Expiry (T): The date the option expires. After this it's worthless.
  • Premium: The price you pay to buy the option.
  • Underlying (S): The stock, index, or asset the option is based on.
  • ITM / ATM / OTM: In-, at-, or out-of-the-money — whether exercising now would be profitable.

American vs European

European options can only be exercised at expiry. American options can be exercised any time up to expiry. Most listed equity options in the US are American; most index options are European.

How Options are Priced

The most famous model is Black-Scholes, which prices a European option using five inputs: spot price (S), strike (K), time to expiry (T), risk-free rate (r), and volatility (σ). The key insight is that you can replicate the option payoff by dynamically trading the stock — so the option must trade at that replication cost or arbitrage exists.

Volatility (σ) is the most important and the only unobservable input. Implied volatility is the σ the market is pricing in — back-solved from observed option prices. Higher IV = more expensive options.

Payoff Diagrams

A payoff diagram shows your profit or loss at expiry as a function of the underlying price. The kinked line is the key visual — options have asymmetric payoffs.

Long Call

You pay premium upfront. At expiry: if S > K, payoff = S − K. If S ≤ K, payoff = 0. Max loss = premium paid. Upside is unlimited.

Long Put

You pay premium upfront. At expiry: if S < K, payoff = K − S. If S ≥ K, payoff = 0. Max loss = premium. Profits as stock falls toward zero.

Covered Call

You own the stock and sell a call. You collect the premium and cap your upside at K. Common income strategy.

Protective Put

You own the stock and buy a put. You pay premium but put a floor on losses. Insurance for your portfolio.

The Greeks

Greeks measure how an option's price changes with respect to different inputs. Traders use them to understand and hedge risk.

Delta (Δ)

How much the option price moves per $1 move in the stock. Call delta: 0 to 1. Put delta: −1 to 0. ATM options have delta ≈ ±0.5. Delta is also the approximate probability of expiring ITM.

Gamma (Γ)

Rate of change of delta. High gamma means delta changes rapidly — your hedge needs frequent rebalancing. Gamma is highest for ATM options near expiry.

Theta (Θ)

Time decay — how much value the option loses per day as expiry approaches. Long options lose value over time (negative theta). Short options gain (positive theta).

Vega (ν)

Sensitivity to implied volatility. Long options gain value when IV rises (positive vega). Short options lose. Vega is highest for longer-dated options.

Rho (ρ)

Sensitivity to interest rates. Calls have positive rho (benefit from rising rates), puts have negative rho. Generally small except for very long-dated options.

Benefits of Options

  • Leverage: Control a large position for a fraction of the cost of buying shares outright.
  • Defined risk (long options): Max loss is limited to premium paid — you can never lose more.
  • Hedging: Puts protect a long portfolio. Collars cap both upside and downside.
  • Income generation: Selling covered calls generates income on shares you already own.
  • Flexibility: Strategies exist for every market view — bullish, bearish, neutral, volatile, quiet.

Risks of Options

  • Time decay: Long options lose value every day. You can be right on direction but still lose if you're too early.
  • Volatility risk: IV can collapse after you buy, crushing option value even if the stock moves your way.
  • Short option risk: Selling naked calls or puts has unlimited (or very large) downside. Never suitable without full understanding.
  • Liquidity: Wide bid-ask spreads in illiquid options can make entry and exit costly.
  • Complexity: Greeks, skew, term structure, early exercise — options have many moving parts that can surprise new traders.

Who Uses Options?

  • Retail investors: Protective puts, covered calls, directional bets.
  • Hedge funds: Volatility trading, event-driven strategies, portfolio hedging.
  • Banks / Structured products: Autocalls, capital-protected notes, worst-of baskets — embedded options sold to clients.
  • Corporates: FX and commodity options to hedge business risk.
